Public Sector Accounting · Funds of the Federation

The Consolidated Revenue Fund

What feeds it, what it pays for, how money legally leaves it, and how it relates to the Development Fund, the Contingency Fund and the Federation Account.

Basis: 1999 Constitution s.80(1)Managed by: Accountant‑General of the FederationReported in: Statement of Consolidated Revenue Fund
§80(1)Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999

What the CRF is

The Consolidated Revenue Fund (CRF) is the Federal Government's own central account. Every revenue the Federation collects is paid into it, except revenue a specific law has earmarked for another fund.

Definition

A self-balancing account into which all revenue of the Federation is paid unless another fund has been established by law to receive it, and out of which no money may be withdrawn except as authorised by an Appropriation Act, a Supplementary Appropriation Act, or a law made under s.81 — the constitutional basis for the entire warrant system below.

The commonest confusion in this whole area

The Federation Account (s.162) and the Consolidated Revenue Fund (s.80) are not the same thing, and examiners lean on this hard.

s.162 · Federation Account

The shared pool. Centrally-collected revenue lands here first, then splits three ways by the vertical formula — Federal, State, Local.

feeds →
s.80 · Consolidated Revenue Fund

The Federal Government's own account. Its Federation Account share lands here, alongside every other Federal revenue.

Out · Charges against the fund

What the CRF finances

Every Naira leaving the CRF falls into one of three groups — and the second group is the one worth memorising by name, since it is charged directly, outside the annual appropriation process entirely.

1 · Recurrent expenditure

Every recurrent head in the approved annual estimates — personnel cost, overhead cost, debt service — released through the ordinary warrant system.

2 · Statutory (first-charge) officers — no appropriation needed

Salaries and consolidated allowances that are a direct charge on the CRF, protected from the ordinary budget process to protect the office-holder's independence:

  • The President; Justices of the Court of Appeal; the Chief Judge and Justices of the Federal High Court and the Supreme Court
  • The Auditor-General for the Federation
  • The Independent National Electoral Commission (INEC)
  • The Police Service Commission · Public Service Commission · Public Complaints Commission
  • The Nigerian Law Reform Commission
Why it matters

The Auditor-General audits the very officers who control appropriation — so his own salary is deliberately placed beyond their reach, charged directly on the CRF under s.84(3), not subject to the annual vote.

3 · Pensions and gratuities

Of statutory and non-statutory officers, including the Armed Forces.

§82 · How money legally leaves

Getting cash out: the warrant system

An Appropriation Act is a legal authority to spend — it does not itself move cash. A warrant, issued by the Minister of Finance, is what actually releases funds from the CRF to an officer controlling a vote. Every warrant is issued in duplicate: the original to the Accountant-General, the copy to the Auditor-General, and notice is gazetted.

1
Provisional General Warrant (PGW)

Issued before the Appropriation Act, under s.82 — keeps essential services running on last year's scale, for a maximum of six months or until the Act takes effect, whichever comes first.

2
Annual General Warrant (AGW)

Issued after the Act — the main release for personal emoluments and services in the approved budget.

3
Supplementary General Warrant (SGW)

For additional emoluments/services provided for in the approved supplementary estimates.

4
Reserve Expenditure Warrant (REW)

Releases items the Minister deliberately withheld from the AGW/SGW for special control.

5
Virement Warrant

Moves savings between sub-heads of the same head. Cannot create a new sub-head or raise the total appropriated — that needs a further Act.

6
Supplementary (Contingencies) Warrant

For urgent needs that cannot wait for a Supplementary Appropriation Act. First a Contingencies Fund Warrant moves money into the CRF from the Contingency Fund; only then is expenditure authorised against the relevant head.

Capital expenditure never draws on the CRF at all — it is released separately, by a Development Fund General Warrant, against the Development Fund below.

§14.1 · Reporting

How the CRF shows up in the accounts

The Statement of Consolidated Revenue Fund is one of the statutory statements required under Cash Basis IPSAS: opening balance, revenue for the period, recurrent expenditure, transfers out (to the Development Fund, to the Contingency Fund), and the closing balance carried forward. Its capital counterpart is the Statement of Capital Development Fund.

Related · The other three funds

Where CRF money goes next

Three funds sit alongside the CRF in the constitutional scheme. Two of them are fed from it.

Development Fund s.81(2) · also Finance (Control & Management) Act 1958 s.25

Finances: capital development — roads, bridges, dams, barracks, staff housing, hospitals, vehicles; loans to State Governments for their own development; external financial obligations.

Fed by: a yearly contribution from the CRF, external grants, external loans (e.g. IMF), and internal borrowing — development stocks (long-term), Treasury Certificates (2-year), Treasury Bills (91-day).

Contingency Fund s.83

Finances: unforeseen, urgent expenditure — a natural disaster, say — where virement is impossible and the need cannot wait for a Supplementary Appropriation Act.

Fed by: the CRF. Every advance is temporary: it is replaced by a supplementary estimate as soon as one can be passed, restoring the fund.

Federation Account s.162

Finances: nothing directly — it is a distribution pool, not a spending fund. It exists only to split centrally-collected revenue Federal / State / Local by the vertical formula before each tier's share moves on to its own CRF-equivalent.

§9.5 · The same funds, generic names

Where the "types of fund" list fits in

The Finance (Control and Management) Act 1958 also classifies funds by generic accounting type, independently of the Constitution's own naming. Two of these generic types are not new funds at all — they are the same CRF and Development Fund above, under a different name. This is the pairing worth not confusing them for separate things:

"General fund"

Generic name, s.5 of the Act, for general administration and services. This is the Consolidated Revenue Fund — not a fifth fund.

= is the
Consolidated Revenue Fund

The one and only fund this whole document is about.

"Capital project fund"

Generic name, s.18 of the Act, for acquiring capital assets/facilities. This is the Development Fund — also seen written as the "Capital Development Fund" in the financial-statements literature.

= is the
Development Fund

Covered above — capital expenditure, fed yearly from the CRF.

The Act's full classification runs to eight generic types in total — the remaining six name funds that sit outside the CRF/Development Fund pair entirely, each for its own distinct purpose:

Generic typePurposeExample
Special fundCreated for one specific, named purposeSouth African Relief Fund; African Staff Housing Scheme Fund
Trust fundHeld as trustee; spent only per the trust deedPetroleum Technology Development Fund (PTDF); Research Foundation Fund
Contingency fundUncertain amount and timing of needNatural-disaster relief (s.15 — the same Contingency Fund as above)
Inter-governmental service fundProvides a service to other fundsGovernment Clearance Fund, netting Federal/State transactions
Revolving (working capital) fundFinances a service one unit provides to othersA Revolving Loan Fund
Self-liquidating fundPeriodic transfers in; balance moves to a current fundA Deposit Fund holding third-party money

One level up, the same Act also groups every fund — generic or constitutional — into three broader families by where the resources come from:

  • Government funds — from general tax/revenue powers (the CRF and Development Fund both live here)
  • Proprietary funds — from the business activities of parastatals and government agencies
  • Fiduciary funds — held as custodian/trustee (PTDF, Trust and Agency Fund, Pension Trust Fund)
§9.9 · Ghana's parallel system

Ghana's own funds — and its own confusion pair

The study text also covers Ghana's (unitary, not federal) system side by side with Nigeria's. It has its own version of nearly everything above, under its own names — and its own, very deliberate trap: two funds whose names differ by a single inserted word.

The umbrella term

"Public funds" (Article 175 of Ghana's Constitution; s.5 of the Financial Administration Act) is the generic term for all public money together — the Consolidated Fund, the Contingency Fund, and every other fund Parliament establishes by its own Act: the Road Fund (1997), GETFund (2000), the District Assemblies Common Fund (1993), the Ghana Investment Fund (2002), the Venture Capital Trust Fund (2004), the Business Assistance Fund, the HIPC Fund, and the Social Investment Fund. Every fund on this page is a subset of "public funds" — never a rival to it.

The pair that looks like a typo but isn't

The Contingency Fund and the Contingency Reserve Fund are two genuinely different things, not the same fund written two ways. This is very likely the pairing worth double-checking if a fund-types list ever looked to you like "one name, and the same name with an extra word."

Contingency Fund

The constitutional fund (Article 177). Parliament votes money into it; every advance out of it is later replaced by a supplementary estimate presented to Parliament. Sits under Parliament's Finance Committee, not the Executive.

not the same
Contingency Reserve Fund

A slice of the Executive's own already-approved budget, set aside internally (a fixed % of votes/estimates) as a first line of relief. No Parliamentary supplementary estimate is involved at all — it is entirely within the Executive's own control.

The rest of Ghana's named funds, several of which are the exact same generic type as one of Nigeria's eight (just with Ghanaian examples):

FundWhat it is
Consolidated FundGhana's CRF-equivalent — all central government money except what is legally payable into another named fund, or legally retained by the department/agency that collected it
District Assemblies' Common Fund (DACF)At least 5% of Ghana's total annual revenue, paid quarterly to districts for development projects; overseen by a DACF Administrator — also called the Local Government Fund
National loans fund / Debt service (sinking) fundReceives contracted loans and services their principal/interest — an alternative to holding loans inside the Consolidated Fund itself

Two countries, two systems — don't blend them

Nigeria and Ghana each have their own Contingency Fund, their own CRF-equivalent, and their own fund-type classification. They rhyme deliberately (the study text teaches them side by side), but an exam question naming a specific section number or a specific % figure is always asking about one country's system — check which one before answering.

Six facts worth over-learning

  1. s.80 is the CRF; s.162 is the Federation Account. The Federal share of the second flows into the first — they are related, never interchangeable.
  2. No appropriation act at year-start? The President may still spend from the CRF for up to six months (s.82), via a Provisional General Warrant.
  3. A warrant releases cash; an Appropriation Act only authorises it. Capital spending never touches the CRF — it runs through the Development Fund instead.
  4. The Auditor-General's own pay is a direct CRF charge, outside the budget vote entirely — by design, so the auditor is never financially beholden to what he audits.
  5. Scheduled corporations must remit 80% of their operating surplus to the CRF, on top of ordinary revenue.
  6. The Contingency Fund only ever lends to the CRF — every advance is repaid by a supplementary estimate, restoring the fund for the next emergency.
  7. "General fund" and "capital project fund" are not extra funds — they are the CRF and the Development Fund under their generic Finance Act names. Don't count them twice on a list-all-the-funds question.
  8. Ghana's Contingency Fund and Contingency Reserve Fund are genuinely different (Parliament-controlled vs Executive's own set-aside) — the closest thing in this whole topic to "the same name with a word added", and worth learning as a pair for exactly that reason.
